Women in Design

Throughout history, women have been restricted from many different aspects of society. Laws and social barriers prevented them from “contributing” to society outside the home.

Despite the barriers placed before them, they have persevered. Even though they faced restrictions, women have always been involved in design, often without recognition.

Our goal with this exhibit is to highlight the impact women have had on the design world through many different styles, including architecture, landscape design, fashion, and much more.

This exhibit is a collaborative effort by our Honors Colloquium 390 class, “Women in Design.” This website consolidates all the research collected throughout the semester to highlight women’s work throughout history and their impact on design. We have highlighted important historical information, women in design throughout the years, and many artifacts that caught our attention. Through this exhibit, we want to emphasize that women have always been involved in design, regardless of the barriers in place, and they will continue to be involved, no matter what comes next.

History and Social Reform

1977

All states pass laws to take away women’s right to vote.

1800s

First Wave of Feminism begins, seeking “equality within patriarchal social constructs”.

1831

The National Academy of Design in New York becomes one of the first major institutions to let women study in basic art classes.

1848

Seneca Falls Convention, New York. The Declaration of Sentiments is signed, a plea to end discrimination against women in all social spheres.

1855

Establishment of the Society of Female Artists.

1881

Louise Bethune became the first female architect to open her own firm.

1890

Wyoming is the first state to grant women the right to vote in all elections.

1897

Integration of women in the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris.

1900

All states have legislation to grant married women some control over their property and earnings–modeled after the NY Married Women’s Property Act (1848).

1920

The 19th Amendment to the U.S. Constitution is ratified, granting all white women the right to vote.

1938

The Fair Labor Standards Act establishes minimum wage without regard to sex.

1959

Norma Merrick Sklarek became the first African American woman member of the AIA.

1960s

Second Wave Feminism begins, advocating for “acknowledgement of difference.” Beginning to question other things, such as the built environment and spatial concerns, to try and dismiss inequality and rather embrace differences.

1962

Norma Merrick Sklarek became the first African American woman to obtain an architecture license in California.

1963

The Equal Pay Act passes, promising equal wages for the same work, regardless of race, religion, nationality, or sex of the worker.

1964

Title VII of the Civil Rights Act passes, prohibiting employment discrimination based on race, religion, nationality, or sex.

1965

The Voting Rights Act gave BIPOC women the right to an equal chance to vote.

1965

Weeks v. Southern Bell marks a triumph against restrictive labor laws and company regulations on hours and conditions of women’s work, opening up opportunities that were previously only for men.

1968

Executive Order 11246 prohibits sex discrimination by government contractors and requires affirmative action plans for hiring women.

1971

Reed v. Reed The U.S. Supreme Court ruled it unconstitutional to hold automatic preference for males as administrators of wills. This is the first time the Court disallows a law treating men and women differently. The Supreme Court officially declares women as “persons”.

1974

Cleveland Board of Education v. LaFleur determines it is illegal to force a pregnant employee to take maternity leave on the assumption they are incapable of working.

1974

The Women’s Educational Equity Act (drafted by Arlene Horowitz) funds the development of non-sexist teaching materials, and model programs to encourage full educational opportunities for women and girls.

1981

Kirchberg v. Feenstra overturns state laws designating a husband as the “head and master” with unilateral control of property jointly owned with his wife.

1990s

Emergence of Ecofeminism, shifting the women’s role to outside of the home.

Invisibility and Erasure

Anne Lowe Carolyn Davidson Charlotte Beers Cipe Pineles Eileen Gray Gillian Smith Jeanne Gang Julia Koerner

Ann Lowe

Ann Lowe was a trailblazing African American couturier born into a family of talented dressmakers who built a prominent career designing exquisite, custom-made debutante and wedding gowns for the wealthy American elite. Despite facing racial segregation during her studies, receiving lower compensation than her white peers, and experiencing a lack of media credit—such as when she designed the bridal party garments for Jacqueline Bouvier's wedding—she persevered to open her own New York City boutique. After financial hardship and bankruptcy late in her career, an anonymous benefactor ultimately rescued her legacy.

Today, Lowe is widely celebrated as a pioneering designer, with her intricate, high-quality garments permanently preserved in prestigious institutions like the Smithsonian and the Metropolitan Museum of Art.

Carolyn Davidson

Carolyn Davidson is an American graphic designer who famously created the iconic Nike swoosh emblem in 1971 while studying at Portland State University. Initially compensated just thirty-five dollars for her efforts by company co-founder Phil Knight, she continued employment with the growing enterprise until her workload became too heavy for a single individual.

Years later, following the corporation's public launch, Knight surprised her during a luncheon with a diamond ring and a generous amount of company shares as a token of appreciation. After eventually stepping away from her professional career in the year 2000, she dedicated her time to personal interests and ongoing volunteer commitments at a local medical center.

Charlotte Beers

Charlotte Beers has achieved remarkable prominence as an advertising trailblazer, earning titles such as the most powerful woman in advertising and the queen of Madison Avenue. Her professional journey includes breaking barriers as the first female senior vice president at J. Walter Thompson and revitalizing corporate morale as the chief executive officer of Tatham-Laird & Kudner.

She later expanded her corporate leadership by dramatically increasing billings during her tenure as chairman and chief executive officer of Ogilvy & Mather Worldwide. Transitioning into public service, she worked alongside Secretary of State Colin Powell as the under secretary for public diplomacy and public affairs, earning the prestigious Distinguished Service Medal.

Cipe Pineles

Cipe Pineles was a trailblazing twentieth-century designer who broke barriers as the first female member of The Art Directors Club and a pioneering art director for major publications like Glamour, Seventeen, and Charm.

Throughout her career, she transformed mainstream magazines by incorporating fine art illustrations and later shifted her focus toward shaping the visual identity of Lincoln Center as a design consultant. In addition to her professional achievements, she shared her expertise as an educator by joining the faculty at the Parsons School of Design to direct publication design. Her extensive creative legacy was ultimately preserved when her children donated her archival collection to the Rochester Institute of Technology in 1991.

Eileen Gray

Kathleen Eileen Moray Smith Gray, known as Eileen Gray since 1893, grew up in Ireland and London, and inherited from her father, painter Maclaren Smith, her passion for the artistic world, and her interest in decoration from her mother. In 1901, she enrolled in the Slade School of Fine Arts of London and she also attended the Colarossi School and the Julian Academy in Paris. In 1906 she started her commercial relationship with the Japanese lacquer technician Seizo Sougarawa and shortly afterwards she got engaged in the world of fabrics and carpets in Morocco.

She held her first exhibition in 1913 in the Salon des Artistes Décorateurs, showing decorative panels. It was in 1922 that she opened her own gallery, called Jéan Désert, which included pieces of furniture and carpets. In 1932 she started to design her own house, Temple a Pailla in France, which would be looted and destroyed during the Second World War. Even though she worked with architectural design alongside Badovici, she was actually known as interior and furniture designer, –like her popular Bibendum chair. In 1973 the worldwide rights to manufacture and distribute her designs were granted to Aram Designs in London.

Gillian Smith

Gillian Crampton Smith is a pioneering figure in interaction design whose career bridges traditional graphic arts and digital software development. After studying at Cambridge, she recognized how design principles could improve software interfaces and established pioneering computer studios and graduate programs at institutions like St Martin's School of Art and the Royal College of Art.

She forged vital partnerships with major technology firms such as Apple and Interval Research to foster collaboration between designers and engineers. Her leadership expanded globally when she directed the Interaction Design Institute Ivrea and later helped establish similar academic tracks at the Iuav University of Venice. Honored with a Lifetime Achievement in Practice Award by the ACM, she has continually shaped the educational landscape of interaction design across Europe.

Jeanne Gang

Architect Jeanne Gang is the founding partner of Studio Gang, an international architecture and urban design firm with offices spanning major global cities. Her design philosophy draws inspiration from ecological systems to create striking, community-focused spaces, cultural centers, and high-rise towers that connect people with their environments.

Alongside her built works, she actively researches and publishes on topics like urban biodiversity and building reuse, while also championing workplace equity by successfully eliminating the gender wage gap within her own company. Her pioneering contributions have earned her widespread recognition, including prestigious international awards, a professorship at the Harvard Graduate School of Design, and a designation as one of the world's most influential people by TIME magazine.

Julia Koerner

Julia Koerner is an award-winning Austrian designer and Associate Adjunct Professor at UCLA Architecture and Urban Design, where she also directs the summer programs. Internationally renowned as a pioneer in 3D printing, her cross-disciplinary portfolio includes notable collaborations on Oscar-winning costumes for Marvel's "Black Panther" films and high-fashion projects with Iris Van Herpen and Chanel.

She runs her own practices, JK Design and JK3D, focusing on sustainable and organic digital fabrication across architecture, fashion, and home decor. Her extensive academic research centers on sustainable 3D-printed building facades, and her physical designs have been exhibited globally in prominent museums and featured in leading publications.
